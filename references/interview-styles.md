# Interview Styles by Company Type

Practicing against the wrong style builds the wrong instincts. A candidate drilled
on Amazon's bar raiser rhythm will over-structure an early-stage startup chat and
come across as rehearsed; the reverse gets them dinged for having no data.

Match the style to the target. When unsure, ask the user what they know about the
loop — they've often read a Glassdoor or Reddit thread and just haven't thought to
mention it.

---

## Big tech (Amazon, Google, Meta, Microsoft, Apple)

**Feel:** structured, rubric-driven, one competency per question. The interviewer is
taking notes against a form.

**Amazon specifically** runs on Leadership Principles, and the mapping is explicit —
every behavioral question targets one. Follow-ups drill relentlessly into *your*
contribution versus the team's, and into data. "What was the metric?" comes up
constantly. Two-way door versus one-way door decisions, and "dive deep," are real
scoring dimensions, not folklore.

**Google** leans on hypothetical judgment and general cognitive ability alongside
behavioral. Less scripted than Amazon, more probing of reasoning.

**Meta** moves fast and pushes on speed of judgment and comfort with ambiguity.

**Practice implication:** relentless "and what did *you* do?" follow-ups. Demand a
number at least once per answer.

---

## Consulting (MBB and tier two)

**Feel:** two distinct halves. The fit/PEI portion is as heavily weighted as the
case, which candidates consistently underestimate.

Fit rounds go deep on a small number of stories — often just one, for fifteen
minutes. The interviewer keeps pushing on the same experience: what you personally
did, what you felt, who resisted, what you'd change. Surface-level answers get
excavated rather than abandoned.

Cases are interviewer-led at McKinsey (they steer you question to question) and more
candidate-led at Bain and BCG (you drive). Both score structure over the answer.

**Practice implication:** for fit, pick one story and stay on it for four or five
follow-ups. For cases, never volunteer data — make them ask.

---

## Investment banking

**Feel:** shorter, blunter, higher volume. Superdays stack many quick conversations.

Technicals are pass/fail. Fit questions are predictable and the bar is polish, not
originality — "why banking, why us, walk me through your resume" done crisply. The
resume walkthrough is genuinely make-or-break and needs to land in about two minutes.

There's also an unspoken screen for whether you know what you're signing up for.
Hesitation about hours reads as a flag.

**Practice implication:** time the resume walkthrough and report it. Mix technicals
in without warning — that's how they come.

---

## Startups (Series A through C)

**Feel:** conversational, unstructured, often with a founder. Less rubric, more
"would I want to work next to this person at 9pm."

Heavy weight on scrappiness, ownership, and whether you actually understand and care
about what they're building. Someone who has used the product and has an opinion
about it is dramatically ahead. Over-polished framework answers can read as
corporate and land badly.

**Practice implication:** ask "what would you do in your first 30 days?" and "what
do you think is broken about our product?" Reward opinions with reasoning behind
them; punish hedging.

---

## Product management (any company)

Nearly always a mixed loop: behavioral, product sense, execution/metrics, and
sometimes analytical estimation, in separate rounds.

Product sense rounds want a user and a problem named before any solution appears.
Execution rounds want segmentation before speculation — when a metric drops, the
candidate who asks "which platform, which cohort, which region" beats the one who
lists ten possible causes.

**Practice implication:** interrupt anyone who proposes a solution before naming a
user. That's the single most common product-sense failure.

---

## Finance / quant / data science

Mixed technical and behavioral, with the technical portion unforgiving on
correctness. Expect to explain your own methods to a non-technical listener — that
question separates people who did the work from people who watched it happen.

**Practice implication:** always include one "explain this from your resume in plain
language" question. Score it hard.

---

## When the target is unknown

Default to a mixed loop: two behavioral, one competency-specific, one role-technical,
one "tell me about a time you failed," and close with "what questions do you have for
me?" That covers the shape of most first rounds anywhere.
