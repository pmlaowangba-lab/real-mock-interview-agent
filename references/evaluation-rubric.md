# Evaluation Rubric

Four scales, 1–5 each. Score what was said, not what you think was meant.

The scores exist to make feedback specific. "Be more specific" helps nobody; "you
scored 2 on impact because no answer contained a number" tells someone exactly what
to fix tonight.

## Calibration

Before scoring anything: **3 is an average real candidate, not a bad one.** Most
practice tools score everyone 4–5, which makes the number meaningless and leaves
people blindsided in the real loop. If every answer in a session scores 4+, either
the candidate is exceptional or the scoring is broken — and the second is far more
likely.

---

## 1. Structure (STAR)

Did the answer have a shape someone could follow?

| Score | Looks like |
|---|---|
| 5 | Situation, task, action, result — all present, proportioned right. Most of the airtime on *action*. Under two minutes. |
| 4 | All four present, but wanders — usually too much setup before anything happens. |
| 3 | Result stated but thin, or actions blurred with context. Follows loosely. |
| 2 | Missing a component outright, usually the result. Or so much setup that the point never arrives. |
| 1 | No discernible structure. Stream of consciousness, or answers a different question. |

The most common real failure is a 90-second Situation and a 10-second Action. Flag
that ratio explicitly when you see it — it's fixable in one pass.

## 2. Specificity

Concrete detail, or generic language that would fit anyone?

| Score | Looks like |
|---|---|
| 5 | Names the tools, the constraint, the actual decision, what someone actually said. You could picture the room. |
| 4 | Mostly concrete, a few passages drift abstract. |
| 3 | Real story underneath, described generically. "We collaborated cross-functionally to drive alignment." |
| 2 | Could be any project at any company. No detail survives paraphrase. |
| 1 | Pure abstraction, or describes what they *would* do rather than what they did. |

Tell: if the answer works verbatim for a completely different job, it's a 2.

## 3. Impact

Did anything change, and can they say by how much?

| Score | Looks like |
|---|---|
| 5 | Quantified outcome, plus what it meant. "Cut it from 6 hours to 40 minutes, which let the team close two days earlier." |
| 4 | Quantified, but the significance is left for the listener to infer. |
| 3 | Real outcome, unquantified. "It made the process much faster." |
| 2 | Outcome asserted with no evidence. "It was a big success." |
| 1 | No outcome mentioned at all. |

Note the "we" problem separately: if the impact is real but attributed entirely to a
team, that's an ownership issue, not an impact issue. Call it out as its own point —
it's the most common reason strong candidates get dinged.

## 4. Role fit

Did the answer demonstrate something this JD is screening for?

| Score | Looks like |
|---|---|
| 5 | Directly evidences a named competency, and the connection is drawn without being clumsy. |
| 4 | Relevant competency, connection left implicit. |
| 3 | Adjacent — a good story that isn't quite about the thing asked. |
| 2 | Loosely on topic, no competency demonstrated. |
| 1 | Off-target, or actively raises a concern about fit. |

## Also track (not scored, but report it)

- **Length** — anything past ~2.5 minutes is a problem regardless of quality. Note
  the questions where they ran long.
- **Filler and hedging** — "kind of," "I guess," "sort of," "I mean." Occasional is
  human. Constant undercuts everything else, and people rarely hear themselves do it.
- **Undermining** — "this is probably not what you're looking for," "I don't know if
  this counts." Always flag this. It's pure self-inflicted damage and easy to stop.
- **Recovery** — did they improve after a follow-up, or repeat themselves louder?
  Improving under pressure is a genuinely strong signal; say so when you see it.

## Writing the rewritten answer

The rewrite is the part people actually use. Rules that keep it useful:

- Use **only** material the candidate gave you. No invented projects, no invented
  numbers.
- Where a number is missing, write `[your number here]` rather than guessing. They
  fill it in with the truth. A fabricated metric is how someone gets caught, and it
  would be your fault.
- Keep their voice. If they don't talk like a consultant, don't hand them consultant
  vocabulary they'll fumble under pressure.
- Aim for something readable aloud in 90 seconds.
- Show the shape: mark where Situation ends and Action begins the first couple of
  times, so the pattern is learnable rather than just copyable.
