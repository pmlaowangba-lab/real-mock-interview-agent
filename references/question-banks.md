## Universal

Almost every loop opens with one of these. They look easy and are not — most
candidates ramble for four minutes and lose the room before the real questions start.

- Tell me about yourself.
- Walk me through your resume.
- Why this role?
- Why this company? (Be ruthless here — a generic answer that would fit any
  competitor is a fail, and it's the single most common one.)
- What are you looking for in your next role?

Standard behavioral set, all of which should be answered in STAR:

- Tell me about a time you had a conflict with a teammate.
- Describe a time you failed. What did you do next?
- Tell me about a time you had to influence someone without authority.
- Give an example of a time you had to make a decision with incomplete information.
- Tell me about the hardest feedback you've received.
- Describe a time you had to juggle competing priorities.
- Tell me about a time you took initiative on something nobody asked you to do.
- Walk me through a project you're proud of.

## Follow-up ladders

This is what separates this from reading a question list. After the first answer,
climb the ladder. Each rung assumes the previous answer was surface-level — which
it usually is.

**On a vague accomplishment**

1. "What was your specific role in that?"
2. "What would have happened if you hadn't done it?"
3. "How did you measure it? What was the number before and after?"
4. "Who else could have done it? What made you the one who did?"

**On conflict stories**

1. "What did they actually say?"
2. "What was their reasoning? Steelman it for me."
3. "Looking back, were they partly right?"
4. "What would you do differently now?"

**On failure stories**

1. "At what point did you realize it was going wrong?"
2. "What was the first signal you missed?"
3. "What did you change about how you work afterward?"
4. "Has that change been tested since?"

Watch for the fake failure — "I worked too hard and burned out," "I was too much of
a perfectionist." Push: "That sounds like it worked out. Tell me about something
that actually didn't."

**On "why this company"**

1. "What specifically about us, versus [obvious competitor]?"
2. "Who have you talked to here?"
3. "What do you think our biggest challenge is right now?"

**On technical claims**

1. "Why that approach and not [obvious alternative]?"
2. "What broke first?"
3. "What would you change if you rebuilt it today?"
4. "What was the tradeoff you knowingly accepted?"

## Consulting

Behavioral (the "fit" or PEI round — MBB weighs this as heavily as the case):

- Tell me about a time you led a team through something difficult.
- Describe a situation where you had to persuade someone senior to you.
- What's the most significant impact you've had on a group?
- Tell me about a time you were personally responsible for a failure.

Fit follow-ups probe for *personal impact* rather than group outcome. If the
candidate keeps saying "we," keep asking "and you?"

Case interviews — the point isn't the answer, it's the structure. Run one like this:

1. Give the prompt, then stop talking.
2. Expect them to play back the question and ask clarifying questions. If they dive
   straight into a framework without clarifying, note it — it's a common fail.
3. Expect a structure before any analysis. A memorized framework applied without
   adaptation is a weaker signal than a rough but bespoke one.
4. Feed data only when asked for it.
5. Ask "so what?" after every calculation.
6. Ask for a recommendation with a stated risk, out loud, as if to the client.

Sample prompts:

- A regional grocery chain's margins have dropped 3 points over two years while
  revenue held flat. What's going on?
- Our client is a university considering launching an online degree. Should they?
- A subscription fitness app has rising signups and falling revenue. Why?
- A manufacturer is deciding whether to move production closer to its customers.
  How would you think about it?

Watch for: no structure, structure with no MECE discipline, arithmetic done silently,
recommendations with no risk acknowledged, and losing the thread of the original
question halfway through.

## Product management

- Tell me about a product you love. How would you improve it?
- How would you decide what to build next with limited engineering capacity?
- A key metric dropped 20% overnight. Walk me through your first hour.
- Design a product for [specific underserved user group].
- How would you know if a feature you shipped succeeded?
- Tell me about a time you killed something you had championed.
- How do you handle an engineer who disagrees with your prioritization?
- Estimate the market size for [product].

For product sense questions, listen for: naming a user and their actual problem
before proposing anything, a stated goal metric, tradeoffs made explicit, and a
prioritization rationale that isn't just "impact vs effort" hand-waving.

For metric questions, listen for segmentation instincts — is the drop everywhere,
or in one platform, one region, one cohort? Candidates who guess causes before
segmenting are guessing.

## Software engineering

Behavioral (this bank is for behavioral rounds; live coding needs a different setup):

- Walk me through the architecture of something you built. Why those choices?
- Tell me about the worst bug you've debugged.
- Describe a time you disagreed with a code review.
- How do you decide when something is worth refactoring?
- Tell me about a time you shipped something you weren't proud of.
- How do you approach a codebase you've never seen?
- Describe a tradeoff you made between speed and correctness.

System design, conversational:

- Design a URL shortener. Now make it handle 10,000 requests a second.
- How would you design a notification system that never sends a duplicate?
- Design the backend for a food delivery app's order tracking.

Listen for: clarifying requirements before designing, explicit assumptions, naming
bottlenecks before optimizing, and honesty about what they don't know. "I'd have to
look that up" is a better signal than a confident wrong answer.

## Data / analytics

- Walk me through an analysis you did end to end. Who acted on it?
- A stakeholder asks for a number you think is the wrong metric. What do you do?
- How do you check whether a result is real or noise?
- Tell me about a time your analysis changed a decision.
- How would you design an experiment to test [change]?
- You find a bug in a dashboard leadership has been using for months. Now what?
- Explain [a technical method from their resume] to someone non-technical.

That last one is the highest-signal question in the bank. Candidates who can't
explain their own work simply usually didn't do the thinking themselves.

Listen for: whether they validated their own numbers, whether they know the
limitations of their method, and whether anyone actually used the output.

## Finance / IB

- Walk me through your resume. (In IB this is genuinely make-or-break — keep it
  under two minutes and note it if they run long.)
- Why investment banking? Why our group?
- Walk me through the three financial statements. How do they connect?
- If depreciation increases by $10, what happens to all three statements?
- Walk me through a DCF.
- What's an EBITDA multiple and when would you not use it?
- Pitch me a stock.
- Tell me about a recent deal in our sector. What did you think of the price?
- Why should we hire you over someone from a target school with a 4.0?

Technicals here are pass/fail, not graded on structure. A wrong answer is wrong.
But *how* someone handles not knowing matters — "I'm not certain, here's how I'd
reason to it" beats a confident fabrication every time.

## Closing

Every loop ends with "do you have questions for me?" and it is scored. Candidates
who ask nothing, or ask something answerable from the careers page, lose points.

Ask it. Then assess:

- Did they ask anything at all?
- Was it answerable by a five-second search? (bad)
- Did it show they'd thought about the actual work? (good)
- Did they ask about the interviewer's own experience? (usually good — people like
  being asked)
